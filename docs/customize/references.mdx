Here, we will walk through how to calibrate your writing agent with your style. You paste a few passages of your own writing, DocWriter works out how you write, and the agent follows that voice when you ask it to help. Expect this to take about **ten minutes**.

In this example, the screenshots use public-domain passages from [Alice Meynell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice_Meynell) (1847–1922), taken from [*The Rhythm of Life and Other Essays*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1276) on Project Gutenberg ([HTML text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1276/1276-h/1276-h.htm)), so you can see a real analysis end to end. When you set this up for your own work, you should paste your writing, not hers. Writing references teach voice, not facts: you can use a [writing rule](/customize/rules) for a standing instruction and an [intended audience](/agent/customize#set-the-intended-audience) to name the reader.

## 1. Add three sources

You should start from the references pill in the header (it says **References not provided** until you add some), or open **Settings**, then **Writing references**.

Paste a passage of your writing, say what it is, and choose **Add source**. Do this **three** times. You want three to five sources: fewer gives the analysis little to go on, and more averages your voice away.

Describe each passage in your own words — e.g., "introduction of a paper", "related work section", "a post I like the tone of". The specialists read your description beside the passage, so you should label an introduction as an introduction rather than leaving it as unlabeled prose.

In this example, we pasted three passages from that volume and labeled them:

1. **Opening of an essay on recurrence** — from ["The Rhythm of Life"](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1276/1276-h/1276-h.htm) (also on [Quotidiana](https://essays.quotidiana.org/meynell/rhythm_of_life/))
2. **Character sketch of a reserved man** — from ["A Remembrance"](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1276/1276-h/1276-h.htm) in the same Gutenberg text
3. **Essay on Latinate composure in English** — from ["Composure"](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1276/1276-h/1276-h.htm) (also on [Quotidiana](https://essays.quotidiana.org/meynell/composure/))

You should use a few hundred words for each of your own passages. Longer is fine; a sentence or two is not enough.

Once you have three sources, open the **Analyze** step.

## 2. Run the analysis and wait

Choose **Run analysis**. DocWriter measures your passages, then three specialist agents read them and draft style guidance. A fourth pass combines the drafts. The specialists work from [Leech and Short](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Leech)’s style checklists (lexis, grammar, and discourse — words, sentences, and how the passage holds together), and they use the **model you currently have selected in the DocWriter header** — whatever provider and model you picked for writing.

You can keep writing elsewhere while it runs. On the left, you will see progress: sources prepared, measurements computed, then the Lexis, Grammar, and Discourse specialists, then **Guidance combined**.

You can select any specialist to watch its reasoning, or open **Measurements computed** to see every metric that fired. Each proposition has to quote your writing or it is discarded. Metrics that measured zero are discarded too — e.g., if your passage has no citations, that says nothing about how you use citations.

<Frame caption="Step 2 — you wait while the specialist agents work. Expect roughly ten minutes.">
  ![The Analyze step showing Lexis, Grammar, and Discourse specialists in progress](/images/style-guidance-specialists.png)
</Frame>

## 3. Pick which passage sounds more like you

As propositions arrive, the right column asks **Which piece of writing sounds more like you?** One side is a passage from your sources; the other is the same passage with one habit changed. You react to the two versions so you can keep the habits that sound like you.

You should choose **A** or **B**. If both feel fine, choose **Both are the same to me**. If neither works, choose **Neither is good** and edit one into something you would actually write. You can **Skip proposition** when the habit does not matter to you.

<Frame caption="Step 3 — you pick the side that sounds more like you.">
  ![Preference cards with passage A and passage B for style propositions](/images/style-guidance-preference.png)
</Frame>

Work through the cards until none remain. Reliable propositions can enter your draft without a comparison; the rest wait on your pick.

## 4. Review the style draft and finalize

Open the **Style draft** (or **Active skill**) step. You will see the instructions the writing agent will follow, with short quoted examples from your sources.

Choose **Finalize style** when you are ready. That writes the skill under `.docwriter/skills/author-style/` and tells the writing agent to read it. If you close the dialog without finalizing, your draft stays unpublished. On the Analyze step, **Start over** discards the current draft and its measurements while leaving your sources and any already-finalized skill in place.

<Frame caption="Step 4 — you review the draft instructions, then finalize them for the writing agent.">
  ![The Active skill step listing style propositions with example passages](/images/style-guidance-skill.png)
</Frame>

From the same dialog you can download the skill bundle, restore an earlier version, or remove an instruction you do not want. Once the skill is active, your header pill reads **Style uploaded**.

## 5. Ask for an edit that sounds like you

With the skill finalized, write in the editor as usual. You should put the request in a directive so the agent knows what to do. In this example, we asked for a late train in the rain:

```text
[[ Write two short paragraphs about waiting beside a late train in the rain, sounding like me. Keep the scene ordinary. Replace this directive. ]]
```

Wake the agent, or let the idle timer fire. You will see the proposed edit as tracked changes, with an Accept / Reject / Retry card in the comment gutter. Expand the card so you can read the full proposal before you accept.

<Frame caption="Step 5 — you expand the pending edit and decide whether it sounds like you.">
  ![The editor with an expanded review card for a style-matched rewrite about a late train](/images/style-guidance-editor-edit.png)
</Frame>

Accept when the wording sounds like you. Reject and retry when it drifts toward generic model prose. Your skill stays active for later turns until you analyze again and finalize a new draft.

<Note>
You can download the skill bundle from the style dialog and use it outside DocWriter — e.g., with your usual coding agents that load project skills. The same `author-style` skill files travel with the folder, so other tools can follow the voice you curated here.
</Note>

## Design philosophy

A useful writing skill should contain examples that are:

1. **Unique to your writing** — habits that mark your voice, not generic “good writing”
2. **Actually important to you** — language you strongly prefer
3. **Low effort for you to curate** — easy to confirm without reading a long checklist

DocWriter aims at each desideratum in turn.

**Unique to your writing.** Style guidance starts from a linguistic checklist rather than from vague voice adjectives. Leech and Short’s [*Style in Fiction*](https://www.routledge.com/Style-in-Fiction-A-Linguistic-Introduction-to-English-Fictional-Prose/Short-Leech/p/book/9780582784093) gives a checklist of linguistic and stylistic categories for describing prose — lexis (words and short phrases), grammar (sentences, clauses, and punctuation), and discourse (figures, cohesion, reader relationship, and other voices). Layout features such as headings and lists are kept separately as document conventions. The checklist fits author-style work well because it asks concrete questions about how a passage is built, so specialist agents can propose habits you can verify in your text. Specialists (using the model you selected in the DocWriter header) draft propositions only when they can quote your sources; metrics that measured zero are discarded — e.g., a passage with no citations says nothing about how you use citations.

**Actually important to you.** Building on Shaikh et al.’s [*Creating General User Models from Computer Use*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10831), DocWriter stores what it learns about you as propositions, adapted for a writing setting: each proposition is highly structured for prose and tied to quoted examples. You then refine the draft through interactive comparisons — a passage from your sources beside the same passage with one habit changed — so you keep the habits you care about when you see them in writing.

**Low effort for you to curate.** Instead of rating abstract claims in a long list, you pick between short side-by-side passages (or skip). What you keep is compiled into a skill under `.docwriter/skills/author-style/` for the writing agent to read.

Your local study export (setup time, calibration choices, confidence bands, and timing) lives under `.docwriter/style-study/` and does not include your reference text or generated passages. See [Storage and backups](/help/storage-and-backups) and [Privacy and safety](/help/privacy-and-safety) for where the files sit on disk.
